How the peg pyramid works

The board forms a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row one peg wider than the row above it. A ball dropped at the top has an even chance of moving left or right at every row, and that repeated split shapes the final result. After passing through the rows, it settles into one of the bottom pockets, which are arranged one more wide than the number of rows.

Because each move is a simple fifty-fifty turn, most balls gather near the middle and only a few reach the outer edges. Mathematicians call this a Galton board, and the result follows a binomial pattern. The payout table mirrors that curve: common middle pockets pay less, while harder-to-hit edge pockets pay more. Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket payout is close to the inverse of its probability, then reduced by a small house margin. When the value of every pocket is weighted by its likelihood, the total lands slightly under one, and that difference is the operator's edge. Most versions of the game publish a return to player in the 97% to 99% range, depending on the studio and chosen settings.

A useful reminder: no row count or risk level changes the long-run return in your favour. The settings change variance, not the average outcome.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the final multiplier.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that spreads or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small gap between the true odds and the paid odds that funds the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players check a result later.
Variance
The size of the ups and downs around the average result, which is what the risk control really changes.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify that the path was set before the ball moved.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge prizes become larger while their chance becomes smaller. The overall return stays nearly the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is mathematically better. Low risk smooths the swings, while high risk concentrates value in rare edge hits. Pick the setting that matches your budget and tolerance.
